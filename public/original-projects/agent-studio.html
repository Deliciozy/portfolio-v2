Chapetr 1 - I Refused to Let It Become Just Another Chatbot

Section 1

Kickoff · Stakeholder Interviews · Requirements Gathering

I Entered With Questions Others Hadn’t Asked Yet

At the kickoff, I didn’t walk in as someone new to the project. Weeks earlier, I had built a demo animation for the product director—my first attempt at imagining how scientists might interact with the platform. Because of that, I was already seeing the friction points the team hadn’t named yet.

Everyone seemed excited about a “chat-based AI” approach—simple, efficient, quick to build. But in my mind, I kept thinking: if it’s only a chatbot, scientists will never see this as a serious R&D platform. What they really need isn’t just an input box; it’s the sense of control they have in a lab. That realization shifted my role immediately—I wasn’t there to decorate an idea, but to transform a vague vision into something customers could actually trust.

AI Chat is enough

But how do scientists track the whole workflow?

Section 2

Client Workshop · User Needs Discovery · Workflow Visualization

When Clients Asked for Control, I Realized Data Alone Wasn’t Enough

black-box chatbot

visual workflow

In the first client meeting, I mostly listened. But the more I heard, the clearer it became: the PM’s emphasis on “chat” wasn’t enough. Clients wanted to see. They wanted to visualize the workflow, track multiple tasks running in parallel, and even view snippets of the code behind the agents. What they were asking for wasn’t data—it was reassurance.

Sitting there, I felt a strong urge: I had to translate the AI’s invisible power into something scientists could grasp, like watching an instrument run in a lab. That moment changed how I saw my role. I wasn’t just a designer; I was the bridge between researchers and the AI, responsible for making the unseen tangible.

Section 3

Competitive Analysis · Wireframes · Interaction Flows

I Challenged the Three-Window Idea With My Own Vision

The PM imagined a three-window setup: one for the workflow, one for AI collaboration, one for chat. On paper, it sounded complete. But to me, it felt fragmented—three islands with no natural flow. If we thought it was cumbersome, how would scientists feel under real pressure?

Too fragmented

Simpler, user-friendly

“ Scientists don’t need more screens—they need clarity.

I proposed something different: two windows. A global workflow on the left, a combined chat-and-agent view on the right. This way, scientists could see progress and context together. To lower the cognitive load, I added preset prompt cards beneath the input box, giving users a quick way to start tasks instead of staring at an empty cursor. When I put my sketches on the table, I felt nervous but energized. This wasn’t just about a cleaner layout—it was about fighting for a way of working that made sense to scientists.

Section 4

Design Critique · Information Architecture · Trade-offs

When My Idea Broke, I Learned to Redefine Compromise

The engineers pushed back. Merging the agent logs with the chat, they explained, would contaminate data and compromise results. Hearing that, I felt a sting of disappointment—my neat “solution” wasn’t technically viable.

But instead of defending stubbornly, I listened. I realized my job wasn’t to win my idea; it was to move the team forward. So we returned to the three-window structure, but I insisted on adding visualized agent personas—showing their roles, status, and progress. That one change turned an abstract process into something scientists could track and trust.

Walking out of that meeting, I felt a shift. I hadn’t “won,” but I had earned trust. My value wasn’t measured by whether my idea survived intact, but by whether I helped the team pivot toward something stronger.

Chapetr 2 - When Delivery Pressure Hit, I Chose Ownership Over Shortcuts

Section 1

Moodboard · Hi-fi Mockups · Design Review

The First Time the CEO Remembered My Name

When I handed over two versions of the Hi-fi designs, I wasn’t sure which—if either—would land. One leaned toward minimalism, the other toward a bolder tech aesthetic. The product director took them straight to the CEO and design lead.

Days later, he came back: “The CEO asked, who designed this? Mary?” Hearing that, I froze for a moment. My pixels weren’t just screens—they had become decisions. For the first time, leadership knew my name. That recognition gave me a quiet confidence: my thinking and persistence were shaping the product’s direction.

Section 2

User Flows · Design System Consistency · Team Collaboration

I Became a Designer and a Mentor at the Same Time

With the core pages finalized, a wave of routine admin pages remained. Instead of carrying it all myself, I delegated to an intern. I mapped out flows, explained annotation rules, and showed him how to mark every interaction detail in Figma.

The real reward wasn’t that I saved time—it was watching him grow. He began to deliver pages independently, and I reviewed and coached him along the way. I realized my value wasn’t just execution, but amplifying the team’s capacity. I wasn’t only a designer anymore—I was a multiplier.

Section 3

Design Specs · QA Walkthrough · Developer Handoff

I Took Responsibility All the Way to Development

Handing off to engineering didn’t mean my job was over—it meant it had just shifted. Developers flagged missing states, unclear motion specs, odd gaps. Instead of stepping back, I leaned in. I answered questions, clarified flows, and even recorded demo videos to show animations.

To keep everything aligned, I built a QA tracker: logging bugs, mismatches, and updates through first, second, and third rounds of checks. It was tedious, but necessary. For me, it wasn’t about pixels—it was about whether scientists could trust the experience. That’s what ownership looked like.

Section 4

Product Metrics · Stakeholder Feedback · OKR Alignment

It Wasn’t Just a Dashboard—It Was a 90% Efficiency Leap

Two months after launch, the numbers spoke: R&D cycles cut from 3–5 days down to 3–5 hours. Efficiency up 90%. Over 200 companies onboarded. 2,000+ users registered.

As the team celebrated, I thought back to the kickoff, when I worried if I could handle the challenge. Looking now, my role was never just about designing screens. It was about reframing workflows, bridging cross-functional conflicts, and owning the product until the very end.

The CEO’s words still echo: “This is good. Who designed it? Mary?” That was the moment I knew—I wasn’t just part of the project; I had become part of its story.
