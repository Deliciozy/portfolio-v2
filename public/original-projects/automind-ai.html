Project Summary

I led the design of Automind, the company’s very first product—an “AI second brain” built to help creators and knowledge workers manage scattered files and overwhelming information. Starting as a team lead among design interns, I explored user pain points, defined seven key modules, and guided design discussions under tight ambiguity. From challenging AI folder concepts to delivering a full component system, I balanced innovation with feasibility. Within five days of launch, our waitlist grew to 2,000+ users, validating the product’s resonance.

Section 1

Kickoff · Stakeholder Interviews · Requirements Gathering

The Company’s First Project Asked Us to Design a Second Brain

At kickoff, I realized the scale of our ambition. This wasn’t just a new product; it was the company’s very first, and it carried the lofty promise of simulating a “second brain.” I felt the tension between the bold vision and our inexperience as interns. But seeing how creators struggled with scattered, unstructured knowledge made the challenge feel urgent and real. That urgency, more than guidance, became the fuel that kept me moving forward.

Section 2

User interview · Insight synthesis / Data analysis · Empathy mapping

Their Files Were Everywhere, and Nowhere at Once

Analyzing surveys with the design lead, we saw the same frustration repeated: creators’ files were scattered across platforms, in formats tools couldn’t unify. When they needed something, it became a scavenger hunt through tabs and drives. To me, this crystallized the real problem—not storage capacity, but the anxiety of never knowing where your own knowledge lived. That clarity sharpened my empathy and gave us the responsibility to make retrieval feel effortless.

Knowledge scattered across platforms & Retrieval turned into a scavenger hunt

Section 3

Concept ideation · Problem framing (diverge→converge) · Journey mapping

Smart Folders Looked Brilliant, Until They Became Incomprehensible

Our first proposal—AI auto-sorting files into endless tags and folders—looked innovative, but I pushed back. Users wouldn’t know where anything lived, and categories would shift unpredictably as content grew. That lack of control clashed with how people actually retrieve knowledge. I reframed the idea: instead of static folders, “AI folders” could be dynamic collections born from searches. It turned a confusing promise into a simple truth: make search smarter, not storage messier.

Section 4

Information architecture / Sitemap · Wireframe creation · Workshop facilitation

From Chaos of Ideas, Seven Pillars Emerged

After weeks of brainstorming, I guided the team to consolidate scattered concepts into seven product modules—cards, search, AI inspiration, AI folders, AI copilot, login, and settings. It was messy work, but this structure grounded us. For the first time, the “second brain” was no longer abstract. It felt like building the skeleton of a living system—one that would grow, but only because we had agreed on where its bones should be.

Section 1

Project management · Ownership mindset · Mentorship & coaching

Leadership Meant More Than Delegation—It Meant Guardrails

As B team’s leader, I wasn’t just assigning tasks. Many interns lacked experience, so I created checkpoints, reviewed drafts daily, and gave one-on-one feedback. At first I worried I was micromanaging, but I realized structure gave them safety to explore. The role taught me that leadership is less about control and more about building rails strong enough for others to experiment without derailing.

As team lead, I turned delegation into guardrails

Section 2

Conflict resolution · Agile / Scrum collaboration · Feasibility assessment

The Feature That Dazzled Us, but Died in the Review

Our boldest idea—AI generating new content based on user files—thrilled us, but the product director called it infeasible. Engineers confirmed the generation time and cost would disappoint users. I felt the sting of losing a feature we loved, but I also learned restraint. Innovation only matters if it can ship. The compromise reminded me: being right about the future isn’t enough—you must also be realistic about the present.

Bold, but not feasible—our AI generation feature was cut

Section 3

Wireframe creation · UX strategy · Responsive design

Every Screen Became a Test of What ‘Second Brain’ Really Meant

We finalized modules: simple login, immersive file cards, tags and notes in detail pages, advanced search with shortcuts, AI folders, and a persistent AI chat window. Each screen forced us to define what a “second brain” should feel like—frictionless, anticipatory, but not overwhelming. It wasn’t about flashy AI tricks; it was about subtle details that made users feel their chaos was finally under control.

Section 4

Design system / Style guide / UI kit · Cross-functional collaboration · QA support

Without a Library, We Had to Write Our Own Language

This being the company’s first product, no design system existed. I partnered with another designer to create components, document guidelines, and audit every detail across modules. It was exhausting, but it gave our product coherence—and it taught me that design systems aren’t just about efficiency. They’re how a young product begins to sound like itself. Within five days of our waitlist launch, 2,000+ users signed up, validating not only the idea but the consistency of its voice.

Section 5

User Test / User Experience / Iteration / Redesign

Better user experience drives us to reflect and advance

We made three key adjustments based on usability and visual hierarchy.
（1）We moved the upload button to the same level as the right-side card, aligning it with user attention hotspots and making the action easier to access.
（2）To declutter the homepage and minimize hesitation, we removed folder tags under the search bar.
（3）We fine-tuned the card styles and colors to simplify the interface and create a more immersive, focused experience.
